## Overview

        What started as an idea for a collection of small attractions eventually became a playable vertical slice,

## Concept

<Split ratio="1:1" align="start">
    <div>
        The starting point for `The Kermis` was a fascination with atmosphere.
        I found that term too broad to build a game around,
        so I started exploring what actually interested me about it.
        Eventually, I realized it wasn't simply the atmosphere itself,
        but the memorable moments it can create, and the feeling of wanting to experience them again.
    </div>
    <div>
        During the concepting phase, I discussed a few different directions with my buddy group.
        One conversation in particular made me reconsider the setting:
        if temporality was such a big part of my fascination,
        wouldn't a kermis be a more fitting subject than a permanent amusement park?
    </div>
</Split>

<Center>
    I had explored a related fascination in an earlier project,
    `Weather Report`, albeit through a different approach.
    This time, I wanted to explore that interest in a more interactive way.
</Center>

## Vertical Slice

<Split ratio="1:1" align="start">
    <div>
        I started looking more closely at what made these fleeting experiences memorable.
        One distinction that helped me put it into words was the difference between
        `Vergankelijke Schoonheid` and `Efemere Pracht.`
    </div>
    <div>
        The former is more about the melancholy of things `gradually passing:`
        autumn leaves, aging, or something slowly falling apart.
        The latter puts the emphasis on the brief but intense `moment itself:`
        fireworks, a flower that only blooms for a day, or rain catching the sunlight.
    </div>
</Split>

<Center>
    Where `Weather Report` explored the former, I wanted `The Kermis` to lean towards the latter.
    I also visited the kermis in Burgermeester in 't Veldpark, Zaandam, to experience the attractions and observe the atmosphere firsthand.
</Center>

<Split ratio="1:1" align="center">
    <div>
        My initial idea was to let players spend a day at an amusement park with different companions,
        visiting attractions and taking pictures along the way.
        Eventually, the surroundings and people would disappear,
        leaving the player with the memories of that day.

        I liked the idea,
        but I was still figuring out how to make it interactive without having to simulate every attraction in full.

        Discussions with my buddy group also helped me explore more interactive directions.
        Rather than just witnessing these moments, I wanted players to actively take part in them.
    </div>
    <div>
        This gradually evolved into a collection of smaller attractions and minigames,
        with players deciding how to spend their time.
        At one point, I even considered limiting them to three coins,
        forcing them to choose which attractions to visit.

        The idea was still broader than what I would eventually build,
        but now I had a more tangible direction to work towards.
    </div>
</Split>

## The Shooting Gallery
<Split ratio="1:1" align="center">
    <div>

        I started looking into actual kermis attractions and the different ways they could translate into gameplay.
        I gathered references from several shooting-stall variations,
        including moving targets, shooting games with specific hit conditions,
        and even a few unrelated attractions for inspiration.

        After comparing the possibilities with my buddy group,
        the shooting stall was the one we settled on.
        It offered a straightforward gameplay loop that I could build upon without having to simulate an entire attraction.
        I decided to use shooting stars as the targets, giving the game a little more room for visual feedback and destruction.

<Split ratio="1:1" align="center" reverse>
    <div>
        ### Prototyping the mechanic
        I started experimenting with the shooting mechanic separately from the final stall.
        I adapted a 2D hold-and-release projectile mechanic into my 3D scene,
        using it to explore how the player would charge and fire their shots.

        A big part of this was figuring out how aiming should work.
        I tried several approaches,
        from firing directly along the camera's forward direction to using a fixed direction or aiming towards the mouse position.
        These experiments helped me settle on a more suitable way to aim within the shooting stall.

        I also looked at other games for inspiration.
        The shooting minigame in `Final Fantasy VII Rebirth` was particularly useful as a reference for its
        first-person perspective, timer, scoring and overall presentation.
        I used it to get a better idea of how to frame the attraction as a self-contained minigame.
    </div>

## Systems

<Split ratio="1:1">
    <div>

        ### Interaction

        For an object to be interactable it needs a box collider, because that is what the player raycasts against. Without colliders on the model, the mouse-world raycast went straight through the building and tried to aim into the skybox, where my max reach of 300 made it fail. Placing proper colliders fixed it.

    </div>
    <div>

        ### Camera and game modes

        A main camera with a `CinemachineBrain` blends to any virtual camera it finds at runtime. Enabling the shoot camera switches to it, and disabling it switches back to the dominant camera. That was a temporary, manual solution, and automating it is next.

        When the timer reaches 0, a Unity Event exits shooting mode and re-enters explore mode.

        To stop the player walking off into the void if disabled mid-input, I plan to call `ClearState()` in `PlayerController.OnDisable`.

    </div>
</Split>

### HUD and feedback

The ammo meter was a placement puzzle. Under the aim marker doesn't work with this point of view, so I considered under the timer — since you're watching the time anyway — and bottom right next to the weapon, possibly as a vertical meter. I wanted to ask someone who plays a lot of shooters, or a UI/UX artist, where they'd expect it.

The exploration HUD is intentionally minimal: coins, a time-of-day indication with hard-coded times (16:00, 18:00, 20:00), and maybe a pause button.

## Reflection

The biggest design lesson was learning to distinguish between the idea I wanted to communicate and the experience I could realistically build.

Reducing the scope gave the gameplay the attention it needed, but it also made the remaining gap between the original concept and the finished slice more apparent. The project reinforced the value of testing the actual experience early, then using those results to decide what deserves further development.

The Kermis remains an exploration of how gameplay, atmosphere, and narrative can work together to make a brief experience feel meaningful.

Next, I would automate the camera and game-mode switching, finish the ending sequence, and develop the narrative framing around arrival and departure so the shooting gallery feels like one memory inside a larger temporary place.
