## Overview

        What started as an idea for a collection of small attractions eventually became a playable vertical slice,
        centred around a shooting gallery.

        The project explores the experience of revisiting a familiar place,

## Concept

<Split ratio="1:1" align="start">
    <div>
        The starting point for `The Kermis` was a fascination with atmosphere.
        I found that term too broad to build a game around,
        so I started exploring what actually interested me about it.
        Eventually, I realized it wasn't simply the atmosphere itself,
        but the memorable moments it can create, and the feeling of wanting to experience them again.
    </div>
    <div>
        During the concepting phase, I discussed a few different directions with my buddy group.
        One conversation in particular made me reconsider the setting:
        if temporality was such a big part of my fascination,
        wouldn't a kermis be a more fitting subject than a permanent amusement park?
    </div>
</Split>

<Center>

    `Weather Report`, albeit through a different approach.
    This time, I wanted to explore that interest in a more interactive way.

## Vertical Slice

<Split ratio="1:1" align="start">
    <div>
        I started looking more closely at what made these fleeting experiences memorable.
        One distinction that helped me put it into words was the difference between
        `Vergankelijke Schoonheid` and `Efemere Pracht.`
    </div>
    <div>
        The former is more about the melancholy of things `gradually passing:`
        autumn leaves, aging, or something slowly falling apart.
        The latter puts the emphasis on the brief but intense `moment itself:`
        fireworks, a flower that only blooms for a day, or rain catching the sunlight.
    </div>
</Split>

<Center>

    I also visited the kermis in Burgermeester in 't Veldpark, Zaandam, to experience the attractions and observe the atmosphere firsthand.

        but now I had a more tangible direction to work towards.

        I started experimenting with the shooting mechanic separately from the final stall.

## Playtesting

Once the shooting gallery was playable,
I started getting other people to try it.
The first round of playtesting exposed a few issues with the experience.
Some players struggled to identify which targets were shootable,
aiming didn't always feel accurate,
and the trajectory line could get in the way of seeing the actual shot.

I went back to the hit detection and experimented with different collider configurations and camera angles.
Eventually, I settled on sphere colliders to make hitting the targets more consistent.
I also adjusted the camera's field of view to 80 and made the targets more visually distinct.

The larger stars received brighter,
emissive materials and were worth more points.
This gave players clearer visual priorities,
while also introducing targets that were more rewarding to hit.

The second playtest took place on June 10, with 13 testers.
This time, the feedback highlighted a few additional improvements:
a proper end screen, clearer feedback when the timer ran out,
a faster charge speed and a projectile trail to make shots easier to follow.

I implemented the projectile trail,
but not every suggestion made it into the final build.
An impact marker was also suggested,
as was making more objects in the environment destructible.

I didn't make the entire environment shootable,
but I did develop a reusable hit-response component.
This allowed different objects to react to being shot with optional score,
sound and visual feedback, without having to implement a separate response for every object.
A stuffed animal, for example, could simply play a squeak when hit.

## Systems

<Split ratio="1:1" align="start">
    <div>
        ### Interaction

        I implemented an interaction system to let the player interact with objects in the environment.
        The shooting stall uses this to transition the player from exploration into the minigame,
        without requiring a separate scene or loading screen.

        <Media

            autoPlay={true} muted={true} loop={true}
            caption="Camera transition into shooting perspective."
        />
    </div>
    <div>
        ### Camera transitions

        The project uses Cinemachine to handle the different camera perspectives.
        During exploration, the player uses the regular gameplay camera.
        Interacting with the shooting stall switches to a dedicated shooting camera,
        framing the targets from the intended gameplay perspective.

        I used a Cinemachine Brain on the main camera to handle the transitions between virtual cameras.
        This made it possible to move between exploration and shooting mode without manually repositioning the camera.

        ### Minigame flow

        The shooting gallery has a timer that determines how long the player can play.
        When the time runs out, the game exits shooting mode and returns to the exploration state.

        Following feedback from the second playtest,
        I also added a result screen that displays the player's final score.
        From there, they can either continue exploring or try the shooting gallery again.

        The UI itself remained deliberately functional and fairly minimal.
        It wasn't a major visual focus of the project,
        but it provided the information and options needed to complete the minigame loop.
    </div>
</Split>

## Final Experience

Although the shooting gallery became the main playable attraction,
I still wanted the surrounding experience to communicate the idea behind `The Kermis.`
The introduction and ending frame the game as a visit to a place that's about to disappear.

### The Ferris wheel

I wanted to use a Ferris wheel ride as the cinematic transition into the final moments of the experience.
I first blocked out the surrounding environment in Unity,
using Burgermeester in 't Veldpark in Zaandam as a reference.
I then storyboarded the sequence and provided reference images to a friend who helped create the pre-rendered cutscene.

The sequence moves from a distant view of the Ferris wheel and shooting stall,
to the inside of a gondola, before looking back over the fairground.
In the final shot, the kermis has disappeared,
leaving the player with the view of an empty space where it used to be.

I oversaw the direction and provided feedback throughout production,
while my friend handled the cutscene's environment and final render.

### Narrative framing

The game opens with a reflection on returning to a familiar place on its final day.
The player remembers visiting the kermis when they were younger,
but finds that it no longer feels quite the same.

The ending takes that idea further:

> "You will be missed."
>
> "Yet I'm glad you happened."

It then reflects on the lights,
laughter and games that will be gone by tomorrow,
ending with the thought that perhaps the memories are what matter.

### Trailer

For the marketing assignment,
I chose to create a short trailer rather than a separate promotional website.
The final edit runs for roughly 47 seconds,
split between gameplay footage and the introductory and cinematic sequences.

I wanted to show both sides of the project:
what the player actually does and the atmosphere surrounding that experience.
I also sourced carnival sound effects from kermisgeluiden.nl and edited them into the trailer to reinforce its setting.

The trailer came together close to the submission deadline,
so some of the original audio ideas never made it into the edit.
Even so, it gave the project a concise promotional piece that brought its gameplay and presentation together.

I uploaded the finished build to itch.io ahead of the June 15 deadline,
before presenting the project on June 16.

## Reflection

Looking back,
I think the biggest challenge with `The Kermis` was translating an abstract fascination into an experience that actually communicated it.

I managed to turn the original concept into a playable vertical slice,
and the playtests helped me improve its usability and gameplay.
However, most of that feedback focused on whether the shooting gallery worked,
rather than whether the experience conveyed the feeling I originally set out to explore.

That became particularly apparent during the final review.
One piece of feedback questioned whether the project was actually about `anti-nostalgia`,
rather than the ephemeral splendor I had intended.
The reviewer also pointed out that I had moved from concept to production quite quickly,
without deeply analyzing the kermis experience itself.

It's an interpretation I hadn't fully considered beforehand.
In hindsight,
I had spent more time developing the attraction than investigating what made the setting meaningful in the first place.
I had tested whether the gameplay worked,
but not whether the intended emotional experience came across.

That's probably the biggest thing I'd approach differently next time.
I'd spend more time observing and analyzing the subject before committing to production,
and make room for playtesting the emotional and thematic aspects of the experience alongside its usability.

The project gave me a much clearer understanding of the difference between creating a playable experience and communicating an intended feeling through it.
I don't think those two things necessarily come together on their own.
