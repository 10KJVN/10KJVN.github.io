## Concept

<Split ratio="1:1" align="start">
    <div>
        The starting point for `The Kermis` was a fascination with atmosphere.
        I found that term too broad to build a game around,
        so I started exploring what actually interested me about it.
        Eventually, I realized it wasn't simply the atmosphere itself,
        but the memorable moments it can create, and the feeling of wanting to experience them again.
    </div>
    <div>
        During concepting, I discussed a few different directions with my buddy group.
        One conversation in particular made me reconsider the setting:
        if temporality was such a big part of my fascination,
        wouldn't a kermis be a more fitting subject than a permanent amusement park?
    </div>

## From concept to vertical slice

<Split ratio="1:1">
    <div>

        I started by researching real fairground attractions, with a focus on shooting-stall variations:

        - Shooting nails, where the nails move
        - An RNG game: cutting for €2
        - Shooting fingers: the whole finger has to come off, otherwise it doesn't count
        - A boxing-ball game for inspiration

        The initial ambition was broader, with multiple carnival attractions contributing to the experience.
        As development progressed, I reconsidered how much of that scope was necessary to demonstrate the game's core idea.

    </div>
    <div>

        After comparing them, I chose the shooting gallery.
        I blocked out the stall model and its in-engine setup, then wrote model specifications and art outsourcing requests.

        If the stall was large enough for three sections, I could make three variations, each with one open spot:
        in the first, left and right are occupied so you shoot in the middle, and so on.

        However, I chose to concentrate on one playable attraction instead. That gave me more room to iterate on the gameplay itself, test it with other people, and respond to what made the experience enjoyable.

    </div>
</Split>

## The shooting gallery

<Split ratio="2:3" align="center">
    <div>

        The shooting gallery became the project's main gameplay focus. It offered a concrete place to experiment with aiming, shooting, feedback, and the rhythm of a short minigame.

        I made the star targets breakable in Blender and tested them in game. The first version that worked only aimed linearly, so you couldn't really aim at anything except the middle.

        My checklist for a playtest MVP (29 May):

        - Plastic-breaking sound effect
        - Rifle fire sound effect on every shot
        - Score counting
        - Balancing the stall prefab size and the shooting oscillator
        - A marker or UI element showing where you aim
        - Collisions for the stall
        - Maybe let the cans fall when shot

    </div>
    <Media src="/images/caustics.svg" alt="Breakable star targets" caption="Breakable stars, work in progress" />
</Split>

<Split ratio="1:1">
    <div>

        ### Medals

        I settled on three score tiers:

        - Bronze: 1000 points
        - Silver: 1500 points
        - Gold: 2500 points

    </div>
    <div>

        ### Difficulty

        Difficulty modifiers come from the targets' Rigidbody: mass, and potentially gravity. I managed 2000 points, and reached 2600 more easily with less mass.

        - Mass 1.0: hard
        - Mass 0.8: medium
        - Mass 0.75: easier

        Fairground games are unfair anyway, so I settled on 0.75.

    </div>
</Split>

## Systems

<Split ratio="1:1">
    <div>

        ### Interaction

        For an object to be interactable it needs a box collider, because that is what the player raycasts against. Without colliders on the model, the mouse-world raycast went straight through the building and tried to aim into the skybox, where my max reach of 300 made it fail. Placing proper colliders fixed it.

    </div>
    <div>

        ### Camera and game modes

        A main camera with a `CinemachineBrain` blends to any virtual camera it finds at runtime. Enabling the shoot camera switches to it, and disabling it switches back to the dominant camera. That was a temporary, manual solution, and automating it is next.

        When the timer reaches 0, a Unity Event exits shooting mode and re-enters explore mode.

        To stop the player walking off into the void if disabled mid-input, I plan to call `ClearState()` in `PlayerController.OnDisable`.

    </div>
</Split>

### HUD and feedback

The ammo meter was a placement puzzle. Under the aim marker doesn't work with this point of view, so I considered under the timer — since you're watching the time anyway — and bottom right next to the weapon, possibly as a vertical meter. I wanted to ask someone who plays a lot of shooters, or a UI/UX artist, where they'd expect it.

The exploration HUD is intentionally minimal: coins, a time-of-day indication with hard-coded times (16:00, 18:00, 20:00), and maybe a pause button.

## Reflection

The biggest design lesson was learning to distinguish between the idea I wanted to communicate and the experience I could realistically build.

Reducing the scope gave the gameplay the attention it needed, but it also made the remaining gap between the original concept and the finished slice more apparent. The project reinforced the value of testing the actual experience early, then using those results to decide what deserves further development.

The Kermis remains an exploration of how gameplay, atmosphere, and narrative can work together to make a brief experience feel meaningful.

Next, I would automate the camera and game-mode switching, finish the ending sequence, and develop the narrative framing around arrival and departure so the shooting gallery feels like one memory inside a larger temporary place.
