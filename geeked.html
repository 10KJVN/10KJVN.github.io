About Dirt Jam

The Dirt Jam is a month-long community event run by Acerola to encourage participants to get started learning graphics and shader programming. Due to the low-level nature of Godot compositor effects, you get to explore rendering concepts hands-on without the safety net of fully featured commercial engines.

Intro

Way2Terrain began as my entry for Dirt Jam — a chance to push Godot's low-level rendering and see how far I could take a terrain system in just 21 days. The goal wasn’t to make a game, but to treat the project as both a technical playground and a creative statement.

Mood board

Development

Engines like Unity and Unreal come with mature terrain systems and polished shader workflows. Godot doesn’t — which made this an ideal challenge. From building a modular shader include system, to fixing fog blending issues, to getting triplanar texturing working with mixed texture resolutions, most of the work was about recreating conveniences we take for granted in bigger engines.


Technical Challenges

  • • GPU Data Alignment – Learned that forgetting to pad a struct for 16-byte alignment can turn your shader uniforms into nonsense. GPUs read in vec4 chunks, so a vec3 needs a dummy float to keep the layout intact.

    • Shader Modularity – Built a GDScript-driven GLSL include loader, cutting ~500 lines of duplicated shader code.

    • Texturing – Took 5 days of trial and error to achieve clean triplanar mapping, supporting multi-resolution textures without stretching.

    • Fog & Lighting – Switching to exponential fog revealed blend issues that flattened lit terrain. Lighting was upgraded to Blinn–Phong for sharper highlights.

Conclusion

This project taught me more about rendering pipelines, Vulkan’s API, compositor effects, and memory layouts than I expected going in. My favorite takeaway was figuring out GPU alignment — and coming up with an analogy to understand it mid-project:

“Think of UBO memory like an inventory grid. Each data type has its own slot size, and a row can only hold 4 slots. Bigger items (like vec3 and vec4) get VIP spots at the start of the row, and smaller items fill in the gaps. If the order changes on the CPU side, you have to re-pack the layout to match on the GPU side.”
