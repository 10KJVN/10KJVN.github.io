Development

Way2Terrain came together through a mix of procedural generation, custom shaders, and careful environment tuning. The dreamy look hid a journey of trial, error, and crash courses in low-level rendering.


Core Technical Steps

  • • Procedural Terrain – Generated using layered noise functions (Fractional Brownian Motion) and heightmaps to sculpt organic landscapes.

    • Clouds – Combined Godot’s Sky3D volumetric clouds with hand-tuned 2D cumulus settings for depth and variety.

    • Lighting – Adjusted scattering, Mie intensity, and sun direction to avoid overexposure while preserving a glowing, ethereal look.

    • World Environment – Applied the Way2Geeked palette, contrast tweaks, and atmospheric haze to match the intended vibe.


Low-Level Rendering

While most engines have luxuries like built-in shader includes and automatic shader recompilation. Godot's lower-level approach forced me to understand what’s actually happening under the hood — and just how much these “automatic” features enhance the users' experience (UX).

One of the biggest challenges was learning about Uniforms, Uniform Buffer Objects (UBOs), and the rules of data alignment and padding. I reached out to other graphics programmers in the Dirt Jam community, and after a lot of back-and-forth, we landed on an analogy that finally clicked for me:


“Think of UBO memory like an inventory grid. Each data type has its own slot size, and the row can only hold 4 slots. Bigger items (like vec3 and vec4) get VIP spots at the start of the row, and smaller items fill in the gaps. If the order changes on the CPU side, you have to re-pack the layout to match on the GPU side.”

That “inventory puzzle” understanding was a turning point. From there, I could properly structure my UBOs, prevent GPU data corruption, and finally move on to implementing features like:


  • • Distance Fog – Using Beer’s Law for a natural fade.

    • Water Shader – Fragment-based displacement with Fresnel reflections integrated into Sky3D.

    • Post-Processing – Lens dirt, film grain, and Filmic tone mapping to push the mood.

Conclusion

Way2Terrain ended up being both a technical playground and a creative statement — proof that a game engine can be just as much about expression as simulation. However so the journey wasn’t without heartbreak... most notably, I missed the official submission deadline due to an incomplete itch.io page and a broken build. Still, I walked away with far more than screenshots:

  • Start builds and deployment testing early.
  • Respect the time sink of low-level systems; scope accordingly.
  • Sleep is a feature, not a bug.
